{"a.":"The noun stems include t’oy ‘person’, hų ‘cedar’, pę ‘deer’, ka ‘mother’, p’įw ‘sparrow’, p’ǫy ‘brother’, ci ‘eye’, p’i ‘head’ and ki ‘blanket’. The endings -mų, -mąt’ę and -xi on the verbs mean ‘saw’, ‘hit’ and ‘waited for’, respectively. The material before the verb ending identifies the object and its number; for example, pihų refers to one cedar, whų to multiple cedars, and pit’oy to multiple people.\n\nNouns used as subjects have endings such as -na, -ne and -nemą, with the choice depending on the noun and its number. Possessive prefixes distinguish ‘our’, ‘your’, ‘their’ and ‘his’; their form also depends on the noun and its number. For example, ąmci is ‘his eye’, whereas ąwci is ‘his eyes’.\n\nApplying these patterns gives the following translations into English:\n(a) Their people saw the cedars.\n(b) His deer hit the mother.\n(c) His cedars hit the people.\n(d) The sparrow waited for the deers.\n\nThe translations into Taos are:\n(a) kąmp’įwne ipikaxi\n(b) p’ine ikimų\n(c) kiwcine iwp’imų\n(d) ipęna ip’ǫyxi"}
